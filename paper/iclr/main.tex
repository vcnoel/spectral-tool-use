\begin{abstract}
When a language model agent calls the wrong tool, the call is usually well formed, its arguments have the
right types, and its token probabilities look ordinary, so the runtime executes it before anyone can review
it. A guardrail must therefore judge the call as it is generated, from what the model exposes in a single
forward pass. We compare the published detectors of both internal families, probes on the residual stream
and diagnostics of the attention graph, together with several related detectors, on identical data, splits
and labels across small models of several attention designs and three tool-calling distributions, with
tools held out at test, a length floor in every table, and paired intervals on every difference. Three
results emerge. Whether an internal judge is needed at all is a property of the model family, not of its
size: on some families output confidence is uninformative and a trained internal judge is the only thing
that works; on others confidence already tracks the model's errors and nothing we trained improves on it.
Where internals are needed, probes on the residual stream at the structural positions of the call are the
strongest judge, and diagnostics of the attention graph come within a few points of them, but only when the
heads are analysed separately. We explain why the head averaging this literature performs is the limiting
step: algebraic connectivity is a concave function of the attention graph, so averaging over heads can only
overstate how well connected it is, with equality exactly when every head shares a Fiedler vector, and real
layers sit far from that condition. The two families carry partly independent evidence, yet no combination
of their scores improves on the better one, and we report that negative result with its operating points.
The outcome is a rule stated in terms of the model to be deployed and what its serving stack exposes, not a
ranking of methods.
\end{abstract}

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}
Every number in this paper expands from a macro that a released script builds from the result files; no
value is typed into the text by hand, a macro with no backing result renders as a visible marker, and an
undefined one stops the build. Models and benchmarks are public; one command reproduces each run and one
regenerates every table and figure. Labels are recomputed from the stored generations at evaluation time.
Generation is greedy and seeded, in bfloat16 with deterministic kernels and a pinned chat-template date, and
every dump records library versions, hardware and commit. Two independent extractions of one model and
benchmark differ by \replItemsDeltaBaseLlamaOneBGlaive{} labels and by at most
\replMaxAucDeltaBaseLlamaOneBGlaive{} AUC on any detector (\replMaxAucDetectorBaseLlamaOneBGlaive{}); we
report this drift (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}) rather than assume it away, and no difference smaller than it is
claimed in the text. Appendix~\ref{app:validity} lists the validity defects found in our own measurement
chain, each with the artefact it produced and the guard that now prevents it.

\subsubsection*{Ethics statement}
The study measures open-weight language models on public tool-calling benchmarks. No model was trained or
fine-tuned, no capability is elicited, and no human subjects or personal data are involved. The work is
defensive: its aim is to let an operator stop a wrong tool call before it executes. The same detectors could
in principle guide the construction of calls that evade them, which is why we report operating points, the
architectures on which each detector weakens, and the model family that never abstains when no tool applies,
rather than a single headline number.
